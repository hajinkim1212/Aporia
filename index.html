About

What Aporia is for

Aporia is the Greek word for an impasse: the point at which a line of thought can go no further. We treat that point as the start of careful inquiry, not as a failure.

It comes from a-, “without,” and póros, “passage.” Our work begins when a question resists an easy answer and a student has to decide how to proceed.

Mission

Aporia helps students develop their own questions, investigate them rigorously, examine assumptions, and revise their understanding in light of evidence and reasoning.

We work across philosophy, literature, psychology, history, the social sciences, mathematics, and the sciences. Each field has its own methods and standards of evidence, and members are expected to respect those differences.

The cycle of inquiry

  1. Record your initial positionBefore researching, state what you believe, why, and how confident you are.
  2. Formulate the questionNarrow a general interest into one answerable question, and decide what evidence could answer it.
  3. InvestigateGather and evaluate sources, write a draft, and report on difficulties along the way.
  4. Receive peer reviewA reviewer states the claim as understood, the weakest evidence, and the most important missing objection.
  5. Document the revisionCompare the final work with the initial position. State what changed, what did not, and why.

Standards

  • Clarity of the questionState precisely what is being investigated.
  • Appropriateness of evidenceUse sources and methods suited to the kind of claim.
  • Separation of fact and interpretationDistinguish what is established, inferred, and conjectured.
  • Openness to criticismConsider objections and explain revisions made in response.
  • Acknowledgment of limitsIdentify what the sources and methods cannot show.
  • Honest publicationDisclose sources and each author's contribution.

A note on review

Peer review at Aporia is conducted by students. It does not certify that a work is free of error and is not a substitute for review by subject experts. Published work is always labeled accordingly.

Research Essay

Philosophy · Cognitive Science · Literature

Who Edits the Editor?

Author Hajin KimPublished October 11, 2026Type Research Essay

Central question

If the perspective through which we understand ourselves is itself shaped by the past we are trying to understand, how can we recognize and correct errors in our self-understanding?

Guiding question: Who edits the editor when the editor is part of the story?

AbstractWe often understand ourselves through memories of past experiences, yet memory is neither a perfect record nor an interpretation-free account of the past. Drawing on Jorge Luis Borges's Funes the Memorious and research on reconstructive memory, selective forgetting, and autobiographical memory, this essay examines how people transform remembered experiences into explanations of who they are. It proposes an editorial process consisting of four operations: selection, abstraction, interpretation, and narrative reconstruction. However, the same processes that make self-understanding possible can also preserve inaccurate beliefs. The essay therefore asks how people can correct their self-understanding when the perspective they use to evaluate themselves is itself shaped by their past. It argues that complete self-knowledge may remain unattainable, but more reliable self-understanding is possible through evidence, alternative explanations, and a willingness to revise one's interpretations.

Reflection on the Inquiry

What Remains Uncertain?

Although the editorial process offers a way to understand how people construct and revise their self-understanding, it does not establish a universal method for identifying every mistaken belief. Further questions remain: How can we distinguish a meaningful pattern from one created by selective attention? When should external evidence outweigh our own recollections? And how can we evaluate competing interpretations when the available evidence supports more than one explanation?

These questions suggest that correcting self-understanding is not a problem that can be solved once and for all. It is an ongoing process of examining the relationship between experience, interpretation, and evidence.

A Question for Further Inquiry

If every attempt to understand ourselves begins from a perspective shaped by previous experience, what standards should we use to decide whether one interpretation of ourselves is more reliable than another?

This question remains open for further investigation.

Contribute

Bring a question

Anyone in the community may propose a question, write an inquiry, or review the work of others. You do not need prior research experience.

What you can submit

  • Research reportAn original investigation of a question, using sources suited to the field.
  • EssayA sustained argument that takes objections seriously.
  • Literature reviewA critical survey of what is known about a question.
  • Critique or question analysisAn examination of an argument, a claim, or the question itself.

What every submission includes

  • An initial positionWhat you believed before researching, and why.
  • A refined questionOne sentence, with the kind of evidence that would answer it.
  • SourcesFull citations for everything you relied on.
  • A statement of limitsWhat your sources and methods cannot show.
  • A revision noteWhat changed after review, and what you kept despite criticism.

From submission to publication

  1. ProposeShare your question and your initial position.
  2. DraftInvestigate and write, with a short interim report.
  3. Peer reviewA reviewer answers three questions about your claim, evidence, and missing objections.
  4. ReviseRespond to the review and record what you accepted and what you did not.
  5. PublishThe editors check clarity, sources, and attribution before the piece appears.
